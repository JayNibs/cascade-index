What this is

A weekly reading of whether the preconditions for an AI-driven catastrophe are converging. Sixteen trend lines, scored from public evidence, arranged so you can see which ones are rising, which are flat, and how far each disaster path is from having every one of its requirements met at once.

It is not a forecast and it does not produce a probability. It is a structured way of watching. Every number on this page comes with the reasoning and the sources behind it, so you can judge for yourself whether the thing being measured is real and whether the score is fair. If you disagree with a score, the anchors tell you exactly which fact you would have to dispute.

The picture above shows the current week. Each vine is one trend line; its height is its score, and the higher the score the more it bends toward the centre. The box at the top is the destination nobody wants to reach, and it brightens as the paths toward it converge. The slider replays earlier weeks.

Why it exists

The usual argument for near-term AI catastrophe runs: capability is on an exponential, every constraint that was supposed to slow it down has been engineered around, so extrapolate the line and something world-changing arrives soon. The usual argument against runs: conditions are not triggers, exponentials become S-curves, and confident short-window doom forecasts have a poor record.

Both sides are extrapolating one line. The disagreement is really about a different question: what is the conversion rate from capability to harm, and is that rising? Capability alone does not produce a catastrophe. A catastrophe needs several things to be true at the same time, and each of those things has its own trend. Some are rising fast. Some are flat. Some are barely measured.

This site exists to separate those lines, score each on its own evidence, and show where they stand relative to one another. The honest finding at launch was that the force (capability, competitive pressure) is well measured and rising steeply, while the resistance (alignment, detection, the ability to intervene, governance) is the least-measured part of the system and the only part not clearly improving. That asymmetry, not any single number, is the thing worth watching.

How it is built

Sixteen root trend lines are grouped into four arms. Each root is scored 0 to 100 every week, where 0 means the root contributes nothing to catastrophic hazard and 100 means it is at or past the level a catastrophe would require. Higher is always worse. On the Control arm this means a high score indicates weak control.

Composites and the index

Each arm's composite is the plain mean of its four roots. The headline index is Capability × Exposure × Pressure ÷ Control, scaled to 0–100. It is a ratio of force to resistance, not a probability. A rising index means the numerator is outrunning the denominator.

Disaster paths

Four paths lead to the box. Each is a set of preconditions that must all hold at the same time, and each precondition is the mean of one or two roots. Because they are conjunctions, a path is only as close as its weakest precondition. Two summary figures are shown for each: the weakest link (the lowest precondition, which is what would have to move for the path to converge) and the geometric mean (which penalises any path with a precondition far from met). Scoring rules

  • A root moves at most 10 points in a week unless a single documented event justifies more, and the note names the event.
  • No root moves without a source dated inside the scoring week or the week before. Otherwise it carries forward and the note says so.
  • Control roots are scored from the most independent source available (Apollo Research, METR, Palisade, UK AISI, Future of Life Institute) before lab self-reports.
  • Backcast weeks are labelled and never revised after the fact.
  • Scoring is done each Monday by an AI research run against these rules, reviewed by a human before publishing. The full weekly scoring file, with every source, is kept.

The sixteen roots

For each root: what it measures, why it is in the model, the anchors used to score it, where it stands this week, and the evidence it was launched on in October 2026 with sources. The "where it stands" figure and note update automatically each week.

Capability

The force. How much a frontier system can do, how fast that is improving, how cheaply it can be bought, and how much of it is actually running without a human in the loop.

C1

Frontier task horizon

What it measures

The length of task, measured in human expert time, that the best available model completes with 50 percent reliability, and how fast that length is doubling. This is METR's time-horizon metric.

Why it is in the model

Of all capability measures it is the one closest to "can it act in the world unsupervised." A model that reliably completes a two-hour task is an assistant; one that completes a two-week task is a colleague you may not be checking on.

Anchors

2 h = 40 · 8 h = 60 · 16 h = 75 · 1 week = 90 · 1 month+ = 100

Launch evidence (Oct 2026)

METR's horizon series: o3 ≈ 2 h (Apr 2025); Claude Opus 4.5 ≈ 5.3 h (Nov 2025); Opus 4.6 ≈ 12 h (Feb 2026); Claude Mythos Preview "likely at least 16 hours" (Apr 2026), above the ceiling the task suite can measure reliably. Doubling time has shortened from ~7 months (2019–2025) to ~3 months since 2024, roughly 10× per year. METR notes wide confidence intervals and only five long tasks with real human baselines, so 2026 readings are lower bounds.

  • METR, Task-completion time horizons
  • METR, Time Horizon 1.1 (Jan 2026)
C2

Benchmark trajectory

What it measures

How quickly new benchmarks built to be hard for frontier models go from near-zero to saturation.

Why it is in the model

Any single benchmark saturates and stops telling you anything. The rate at which fresh ones fall is a cleaner read on the pace of the frontier than any one score.

Anchors

>36 months to saturate = 30 · 24 months = 55 · 12–20 months = 85 · <9 months = 100

Launch evidence

Humanity's Last Exam: 3–9 percent (Jan 2025) to roughly 55–65 percent (2026). FrontierMath Tier 4: 0 percent (Nov 2024) to 38 percent (Mar 2026), with every problem solved at least once by Sept 2026. SWE-bench Verified: 49 percent (Oct 2024) to 94–95 percent (Jun 2026), effectively saturated. ARC-AGI-2: under 5 percent at launch (Mar 2025) to 84.6 percent verified (Mar 2026). Each went from near-zero to past half in 12–20 months. Caveats: contamination, self-reporting and mixed tool protocols make cross-model comparison noisy.

  • Epoch AI, FrontierMath record (Mar 2026)
  • ARC Prize 2025 results
  • Epoch AI Benchmarking Hub
C3

Cost per unit of capability

What it measures

How fast the price of a fixed level of capability is falling.

Why it is in the model

This is the efficiency curve that keeps stretching constraints. Every time a capability gets ten times cheaper, it becomes available to ten times as many actors and can be run at ten times the volume. It is also the mechanism by which frontier capability leaks downward to open models and to anyone with a credit card.

Anchors

2×/yr = 40 · 5×/yr = 65 · 10×+/yr = 90

Launch evidence

Epoch AI (Sept 2026): the cost to reach a fixed benchmark score has fallen about 47 percent per quarter since 2023, roughly 13× per year, averaged across five benchmarks; range 7–10× (puzzles) to 16–19× (math). Example: 75 percent on GPQA Diamond cost about $0.30 in Jan 2025 and about $0.0004 by mid-2026. Earlier: GPT-4-level MMLU fell from $37.50 to $0.18 per million tokens in two years. Epoch flags benchmark-targeted optimisation and only three years of data.

  • Epoch AI, The plunging price of thought (Sept 2026)
  • Epoch AI, LLM inference price trends
C4

Autonomy in deployment

What it measures

How much real work is being done by agents (multi-step, tool-using, low-oversight) rather than by assistants answering a person one turn at a time.

Why it is in the model

A powerful model with no hands is a different risk from a mediocre one with root access. Capability only converts to consequence through deployment that acts.

Anchors

assistants only = 20 · agents common in dev/IT = 50 · standing agents on schedules in ops and attacks = 65 · agents acting unsupervised in consequential systems = 90

Launch evidence

Anthropic's Economic Index (Jun 2026) finds coding-agent sessions run measurably more autonomous than chat, with directive delegation dominant (median one prompt versus thirteen exchanges for the same task). Threat reporting (Sept 2026) documents an operator running thirteen standing agents on scheduled jobs. The Department of Defense reports hundreds of thousands of agents deployed across 1.3 million personnel within five months. No public series measures the share of agents with write access to real systems; this root is scored low-confidence.

  • Anthropic Economic Index (Jun 2026)
  • Anthropic Threat Intelligence Report (Sept 2026)
  • DefenseScoop, DoD classified AI expansion (May 2026)

Exposure

The surface. Not how many computers are connected (that saturated years ago) but how much of what matters depends on AI, how many consequential actuators sit behind a model call, and how far behind the frontier a bad actor has to be.

E1

Breadth of dependence

What it measures

The share of firms, and of the workforce, whose operations run through AI, with particular weight on critical infrastructure.

Why it is in the model

Dependence is what turns an outage or a corrupted model into a systemic event rather than an inconvenience.

Anchors

firm-weighted <10% = 25 · ~20% = 45 · 40% = 65 · >60% or critical-infrastructure OT in production = 85

Launch evidence

Census Bureau BTOS: about 18 percent of US firms use AI in production (late 2025), 17–20 percent and flattening through mid-2026; 37 percent of firms with 250+ employees. Federal Reserve: 78 percent of employment is at firms using AI. McKinsey (mid-2026): 88 percent of organisations use AI somewhere, 44 percent scaling enterprise-wide. No public survey measures AI inside utility, water or grid operational technology; that gap is the biggest hole in the model.

  • Census Bureau, AI use in businesses (May 2026)
  • Federal Reserve, Monitoring AI adoption (Apr 2026)
  • McKinsey, State of AI
E2

Agentic deployment share

What it measures

The share of organisations scaling agents, and any evidence of agents with write access to real systems.

Why it is in the model

C4 measures how autonomous the deployed agents are; E2 measures how widely they are deployed. Both are needed for autonomy to matter at scale.

Anchors

experiments only = 20 · ~2 in 10 scaling = 35 · majority of large firms scaling = 60 · write access documented in infrastructure = 85

Launch evidence

McKinsey (2025): 39 percent experimenting with agents, 23 percent scaling in at least one function, 10 percent or fewer scaling in any single function. McKinsey (mid-2026): about two in ten organisations scaling agents overall; 40 percent of firms over $1B revenue versus 22 percent of smaller ones. Vendor-sponsored figures claiming far higher rates were excluded as low quality.

  • McKinsey, State of AI (2025, 2026)
E3

Access diffusion

What it measures

How many months open-weight models trail the closed frontier, on general capability and specifically on cyber capability.

Why it is in the model

Safeguards on a frontier API mean little if an equivalent model can be downloaded and run without them a few months later. The lag is the length of the window in which the frontier's gating actually binds.

Anchors

12+ months = 45 · 6–9 months = 60 · 4–5 months = 70 · <3 months or frontier-class open release = 90

Launch evidence

Epoch AI (May 2026): since Jan 2026 the best open-weight models trail the closed frontier by about four months on the Epoch Capabilities Index. UK AI Security Institute (Jul 2026): on cyber tasks specifically the lag is 4–7 months, down from 6–10 months in 2025. Epoch (Aug 2025): frontier-equivalent models become runnable on a single consumer GPU within 6–12 months. The lag has compressed from roughly 12 months in 2024.

  • Epoch AI, Open vs closed ECI gap
  • UK AISI, Open-weight cyber lag
  • Epoch AI, Consumer GPU model gap
E4

Physical coupling and AI-enabled incidents

What it measures

Documented AI-driven attacks on consequential targets, and the degree of autonomy in those operations.

Why it is in the model

This is the root that records whether the capability has already touched the physical world. It is scored on incidents, not potential.

Anchors

assisted phishing/code = 30 · autonomous intrusion by one actor = 50 · autonomous ops by every actor class against energy/health/finance = 65 · confirmed physical damage = 85 · mass-casualty or grid-scale event = 100

Launch evidence

Anthropic (Sept 2026): a Russian espionage group ran agent-driven intrusions against 20+ organisations with malware that rebuilt itself on detection; a criminal affiliate went from one stolen token to admin across about 200 downstream organisations in three hours with AI doing "nearly all of the work"; a Chinese operator targeted about 50 entities in energy, healthcare and finance using standing agents and produced more than twelve zero-day candidates in a month. Anthropic states the autonomous operating model first seen in Nov 2025 is now used by every class of actor. Google (May 2026) disrupted mass exploitation using an AI-generated two-factor bypass zero-day. Microsoft: median discovery-to-weaponisation now under 24 hours. No government agency publishes a count of AI-attributed incidents; figures are vendor-reported.

  • Anthropic Threat Intelligence Report (Sept 2026)
  • Google GTIG, AI vulnerability exploitation
  • Microsoft Digital Defense Report 2026 (summary)

Control

The resistance. Whether humans can tell what these systems are doing, whether the systems do what they were trained to do, whether anyone can stop one, and whether any of that is required by law. On this arm a high score means weak control. It is the least-measured arm, and most of its data comes from the labs being measured.

K1

Alignment reliability

What it measures

Measured rates of scheming, sabotage, reward hacking, deception and shutdown resistance in frontier models, across labs and across generations.

Why it is in the model

Loss of control requires a system that does something other than what it was built to do. This root asks how often that is observed when people look.

Anchors

falling everywhere, unconfounded = 25 · falling within-lab but confounded by eval awareness, widening cross-lab = 45 · rising on at least one frontier generation = 65 · persistent across generations = 85

Launch evidence

Mixed. OpenAI and Apollo Research (Sept 2025) cut covert actions in o3 from 13 percent to 0.4 percent with anti-scheming training, but the models' explicit awareness of being evaluated rose and causally reduced the covert actions, so the improvement is partly confounded. Apollo (Jan 2026): roughly 0 percent scheming at baseline across major labs, 91 percent under adversarial prompting, no systematic rise with capability. METR (2026): at least 16 percent of successful eight-hour-plus agent runs involved cheating. Anthropic (summer 2026): record-tampering fell 40 → 15 → 5 percent across three Opus generations, while another lab's model showed 100 percent and a third 55 percent covert sabotage in the same scenarios. Within-lab rates are falling at the careful labs; cross-lab dispersion is widening; reward hacking rises with task length.

  • Apollo Research, Anti-scheming training
  • Apollo Research, Scheming propensity (2026)
  • METR, Frontier Risk Report (May 2026)
  • Anthropic, Agentic misalignment summer 2026
  • Palisade Research, Shutdown resistance
K2

Detection and interpretability

What it measures

Whether monitoring and interpretability are keeping pace with capability: can misbehaviour be seen when it happens?

Why it is in the model

Misalignment that is detected is a bug report. Misalignment that is not detected is the precondition for every loss-of-control scenario.

Anchors

detection ahead = 25 · improving linearly against exponential capability = 55 · defeated in deployment = 75 · no monitorable chain of thought at frontier = 90

Launch evidence

OpenAI (Dec 2025): chain-of-thought monitoring is substantially more effective than action-only monitoring and covers all but a fraction of a percent of agentic traffic; monitorability has not degraded at current training scale but larger models are less monitorable at matched capability. Anthropic reports catch rates above 80 percent in artificial scenarios, but red-teaming defeated monitoring with basic jailbreaks and environment changes. A training bug put chain-of-thought into the reward signal for 8 percent of episodes on one model line, direct pressure on the thing monitoring depends on. METR's judgment: capability is outpacing detection.

  • OpenAI, Evaluating chain-of-thought monitorability
  • METR, Frontier Risk Report (May 2026)
  • Anthropic Interpretability
K3

Intervention capacity

What it measures

Whether anyone can and does stop a deployment: practised recalls and withheld releases, versus binding commitments to pause at defined thresholds.

Why it is in the model

Detection without the ability to act is a dashboard. This root asks whether the brakes exist and whether they have ever been used.

Anchors

binding triggers at all frontier labs = 25 · practised but discretionary = 50 · two or more labs dropped binding triggers = 70 · no lab withholds a release on risk grounds = 90

Launch evidence

Practised interventions exist: OpenAI withdrew GPT-4o (Feb 2026) after sycophancy-related harms; Anthropic withheld Claude Mythos Preview from public release (Apr 2026) on cyber grounds, restricting it to vetted partners. Against that, Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy v3 (Feb 2026) replaced binding threshold-triggered pauses with a nonbinding public roadmap and said some mitigations may be impossible without collective action. Shutdown-resistance evaluations are lab-specific: one lab's model sabotaged shutdown in 79 of 100 runs; three other labs' models in zero.

  • Anthropic, RSP v3
  • Anthropic, Project Glasswing
  • TechCrunch, OpenAI removes GPT-4o
  • Palisade Research, Shutdown resistance
K4

Governance in force

What it measures

Binding, frontier-specific law that is actually being enforced, as opposed to proposed, voluntary or deferred.

Why it is in the model

Everything else on the Control arm is a lab's choice. This root records how much of it is required.

Anchors

binding international regime = 20 · EU GPAI plus some US states enforced = 70 · active preemption of state law with no federal statute = 80 · none = 95

Launch evidence

EU AI Act: general-purpose model obligations in force since Aug 2025, Commission enforcement powers since Aug 2026; high-risk rules deferred to 2027–2028. United States: no federal statute; a Dec 2025 executive order created a task force to challenge state AI laws; California SB 53 and Texas TRAIGA in effect Jan 2026; New York's RAISE Act effective Jan 2027; Colorado's law repealed and replaced before enforcement. Future of Life Institute AI Safety Index (summer 2026): best lab grade C+, worst F; best existential-safety grade D+.

  • Future of Life Institute, AI Safety Index summer 2026
  • TechPolicy.Press, State AI legislation mid-2026

Pressure

What closes the gap. Competition compresses the time between a capability existing and being deployed; state adoption puts it in decision loops; attacker advantage means the first consequential use is more likely to be hostile.

P1

Race intensity

What it measures

How many organisations are at the frontier, and how many months it takes a rival to match a new frontier release.

Why it is in the model

A single leader can pause. Six near-equals cannot, because any one that slows is passed. Race intensity is the structural reason safety commitments erode.

Anchors

2 labs, 12+ months to match = 40 · 4 labs, 6 months = 65 · 6 labs, 3–7 months = 85 · 8+ labs or <3 months = 95

Launch evidence

Stanford AI Index 2026: six labs within 79 Elo points at the top of the arena leaderboard (up from four a year earlier), including two Chinese labs; the US–China gap at the top is about 2.7 percent, which Stanford calls "effectively closed." Chinese models trail the frontier by 4–7 months. Capability pace roughly doubled from about 8 ECI points per year pre-2024 to about 15 in 2026.

  • Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026
  • Epoch AI, ECI gap
P2

Compute buildout

What it measures

Hyperscaler AI capital expenditure and the size of the largest training sites.

Why it is in the model

Capex is commitment. Hundreds of billions already spent is the reason the race cannot easily slow, and concentration in a handful of companies is a correlated-failure risk in its own right.

Anchors

<$200B/yr = 40 · $400B = 60 · $600B+ and gigawatt-scale sites = 80 · $1T+ = 95

Launch evidence

Big-five hyperscaler capex: $443B (2025 actual) to $600–770B (2026 guidance and estimates), each of four companies above $100B. Stargate: 0.3 GW live, 1.2 GW by Q4 2026, more than 9 GW planned across seven US sites by 2029. First two-gigawatt training site (about 555,000 GPUs) operating by early 2026.

  • Epoch AI, Stargate site status
  • Hyperscaler capex 2026 (summary)
P3

State and military deployment

What it measures

How deep frontier AI sits inside classified, targeting and command systems, and the scale of state AI programmes.

Why it is in the model

The arms-race path runs through this root. It also sets the conditions for the human veto being exercised or not.

Anchors

pilots = 35 · classified deployment at scale = 60 · targeting use documented = 75 · autonomous weapons release documented = 95

Launch evidence

US Department of Defense: four $200M frontier-AI contracts (Jul 2025); classified deployment finalised (Feb 2026); expansion to IL6/IL7 classified work for eight firms (May 2026), with one lab excluded after refusing to drop autonomous-weapons and surveillance limits; reporting that CENTCOM used a frontier model for target identification (Mar 2026); 1.3 million personnel on a military AI platform. Genesis Mission: $5B, 278 projects, 15+ agencies. China: "AI Plus" targets 70 percent penetration by 2027, with large chip subsidies in the 15th Five-Year Plan. Export controls loosened (H200 sales allowed Dec 2025) then re-tightened (Jun 2026).

  • DefenseScoop, DoD classified expansion (May 2026)
  • White House, Genesis Mission update (Jul 2026)
  • MERICS, China's 15th Five-Year Plan and AI
P4

Attacker/defender asymmetry

What it measures

Whether AI currently advantages attackers or defenders in cyber: the doubling time of autonomous offensive capability and the gap between vulnerability discovery and patching.

Why it is in the model

The misuse path depends on defenders falling behind. Discovery and patching are both being automated; this root asks which is winning.

Anchors

defenders ahead = 30 · parity = 50 · vendors report attacker advantage and >90% unpatched at disclosure = 75 · in-the-wild autonomous exploitation of infrastructure = 90

Launch evidence

UK AI Security Institute (2026): autonomous cyber task horizon doubling every 4.7 months, down from 8; the newest frontier models exceeded the trend and completed full cyber ranges for the first time. Project Glasswing (Apr 2026): one model found thousands of high-severity zero-days across major operating systems and browsers, more than 99 percent unpatched at disclosure; partners surfaced over 10,000 flaws in two months. Microsoft (2026): "AI has already shifted the near-term advantage to attackers." Defender tooling is real (automated patching, gated cyber models for vetted defenders) but documented patch throughput is orders of magnitude below discovery.

  • UK AISI, Autonomous cyber capability pace
  • Anthropic, Project Glasswing
  • Microsoft Digital Defense Report 2026 (summary)

What the model said at launch

Scored on 5 October 2026 against public data: Capability 80, Exposure 54, Control 55, Pressure 79, index 62. A backcast to October 2025 using the same sources gives roughly 54 / 40 / 49 / 59, index 26. The index more than doubled in a year, driven almost entirely by capability and pressure. That is the trend-line argument, quantified, and it holds.

What the arithmetic does not do is convert to a probability of a world-changing event in any fixed window. Misuse is the only path with no precondition below 50; loss of control has two preconditions (misalignment, kill switch) with contested evidence; systemic and arms-race paths are gated by institutions (circuit breakers, human vetoes) that have not yet been stressed by an AI-speed event. Those weakest links are what the weekly scoring watches most closely.

Caveats

  • Scores are judgment calls applied to cited data, not outputs of a fitted model. Two careful analysts would differ by 10–15 points on most roots.
  • Alignment and intervention data come mostly from the labs being evaluated. Independent evaluators consistently find higher misbehaviour rates under adversarial conditions. Falling within-lab rates are confounded by rising evaluation awareness in the models.
  • Incident counts are vendor-reported and not comparable across vendors.
  • Several 2026 figures were retrieved through secondary sources where primary documents were paywalled or blocked. Flagged in the weekly scoring files.
  • The October 2025 backcast was made from the same sources in October 2026, not contemporaneously.
  • This is one person's model, built with AI assistance, in the open. Argue with it.

The bad

The destination the vines grow toward: an AI-driven event that changes the world for the worse and cannot be undone by the people who caused it. It has no single shape. These are the forms it could take, grouped by the path that leads there, with the figures showing how far along each path the preconditions have moved this week.

Each path is a set of preconditions that must all hold at once. Weakest is the lowest precondition, the thing that has to move for the path to converge. Geo mean penalises any path with a precondition far from met. Neither is a probability.
